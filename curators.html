The person behind the photographs

Norbert Bánhalmi’s work, stories and key periods in brief

A note from me

If you are a curator, a director or a collector reading this: welcome, and thank you for the time. What follows is the short version of since 1999 — written to save you an afternoon. If any of it starts a conversation, that is exactly what it is for. My inbox is open: hello@norbertbanhalmi.com

The short version

Discipline, human presence and intimacy run through the same photographic practice. The work moves from the discipline of my military years toward increasingly intimate studies of memory, the body and human connection. The photographs ask how identity changes, how memory is carried, and how the body can remain visible without being reduced to an object. Three projects hold the whole thing up: Milestones ’56, Awakening, and The World of Woman. The work moves along a New York–Vienna–Budapest axis and is also shown internationally on Saatchi Art.

1 · From discipline to human stories

What the military taught me

In 2006 I founded the first professional photo studio of the Hungarian Defence Forces. It taught me to work quickly, precisely and responsibly. Today I use the same attention to notice small, honest gestures. I often work with difficult subjects — illness, ageing or trauma — but I approach them quietly. The technique stays in the background so the person can remain at the centre of the photograph.

New York: the school of seeing

New York is where my eye finally settled. There I learned to see, not just to look. The urban years also produced Sunrise Ride, which won the audience award at the European Environment Agency's international competition, and taught me how to lift a documentary moment to fine-art level.

Less retouching, more reality

Since Real Women (30+1) in 2014, I have consistently avoided heavy retouching. Wrinkles, skin texture and asymmetry are not flaws; they are part of a life. I work with natural or soft light and give people enough time to stop posing.

2 · Historical trauma: Milestones ’56

Made for the 60th anniversary with the Hungarian House in New York — the strongest example of me working as a historian more than an artist. Months of fieldwork across New York State, Connecticut and New Jersey; oral-history interviews; and a diptych concept in which survivors are photographed together with the object they carried out of Hungary. Classical portraiture, Rembrandt light, direct gazes, no victim poses. The project also triggered a genuine historiographical argument about the categories of hero and perpetrator — which, curatorially, only raises its value: I recorded rather than judged, and that raw memory is what real trauma processing requires.

Milestones ’56 →

3 · The body as battlefield and sanctuary: Awakening

Realised with the Semmelweis University oncology team (Dr. Magdolna Dank) and psychiatrist Dr. Imre Csernus, the project brought photography into a medical context. I focused on recovery rather than shock. Several participants described the sessions as an important part of processing what they had lived through. In the black-and-white portraits, scars remain visible as part of the body and its history; poems by Vincent & Vincent accompany the images with an independent voice. The series toured Budapest, Noszvaj and Veszprém and hangs permanently at the Csolnoky Ferenc Hospital Oncology Centre.

Awakening — The New Beginning →

4 · The stations of female existence: The World of Woman

Nine natural transitions, made with writer Miklós Vámos, challenge the narrow expectation that women should appear young, thin and flawless. The project extended beyond the exhibition through an online space where people could submit their own stories; my role shifted from author to host. I used a small camera rather than an imposing DSLR to keep eye contact and conversation natural. At Stefánia Palace, a roundtable with coaches and models turned the finale into an open discussion.

5 · Technology as an artistic tool

  • OM SYSTEM Ambassador (2018–) — The Japan Times covered how I renewed the brand's communication in Hungary around user experience, education and community rather than specifications.
  • First Hungarian photographic NFT collection — With The Men's Dream I was the first to bring Hungarian photographic tradition onto the blockchain, debuting with a charity auction in a Manhattan club.
  • Rege Gallery, Tihany (2020–) — Co-founder and curator of a digital exhibition space built with Samsung, where works were shown on screen in the setting of Tihany’s Benedictine abbey.
  • Sunrise Ride NFT — The EEA award-winning image later paired digital ownership with a physical artwork and an original musical composition.

6 · Toward sacred intimacy: Touch

The 2024 Touch Vienna and Touch Munich projects approach touch as attentive human connection rather than through the objectification of the body. The earlier monochrome language gives way to gold tones and candlelight. The series asks how touch can be experienced again as something safe, attentive and deeply human.

7 · Why this might interest you

  • A credible artistic trajectory — The path from a military background to humanist photography is not biographical decoration; it is visible in the subjects, method and discipline of the work.
  • Human and social context — The work engages directly with emigration, illness, women’s lives and historical memory.
  • A coherent visual language — Restrained composition and a recurring monochrome palette connect the different periods of the work.
  • Technological experimentation — Digital exhibition and NFT projects explore how photography can move through new contexts of presentation and collecting.

Five key periods

How the work changed over the years

These are not five separate careers. The subjects and places changed, but the same question stayed with me: when does a person, a memory or a community truly reveal itself?

Explore the five periods
I · 1999–2013

Discipline, roles and the person behind them

My early work came from paid assignments and formal organisations. Even then I was interested in the gap between the role someone had to play and the person inside it. Clear structure, restraint and responsibility stayed with me.

II · 2014–2016

Personal stories and shared history

In New York, and later through stories about women, fatherhood and 1956, personal memories moved to the centre of my work. I wanted private experience and shared history to remain visible in the same photograph.

III · 2017–2018

The body after change

Awakening, Snippets and The World of Woman followed people through illness, recovery, intimacy, ageing and turning points. Photographs, personal accounts, literature and performance came together, while everyone remained in control of their own story.

IV · 2019–2022

Books, exhibitions and gallery work

Books, exhibitions, brand partnerships and gallery work brought the photographs to wider audiences. My aim was simple: let the work travel without losing the people and stories at its heart.

V · 2023–present

Community, teaching and public portraits

Vienna, teaching, community and charity projects, and portraits of leaders brought relationships even closer to the centre. I want each person to be seen with trust and dignity, never reduced to a familiar pose.

What I look for in a portrait

When does someone truly appear in a photograph?

Simply standing in front of a camera is not enough. A strong portrait grows from posture, gaze, trust, personal history and the brief moment when the role falls away. The next nine points show what I pay attention to while making and preserving an image.

Explore the nine dimensions
1

What the body tells us

The body, the gaze, posture, hands, breath, gesture, and the relationship between light and space. The body is not a formal prop; it carries experience.

2

When the role falls away

The moment when the sitter is no longer performing an expected role. The portrait is not an exposure but a safe situation in which defences can briefly be set down.

3

Trust between two people

The trust built between photographer and sitter. An image is shaped by more than optics and technique; it also depends on the human encounter.

4

A person within a community

A portrait can also be the document of a community, a social situation or an era. A personal image can therefore reach beyond the individual.

5

What a photograph preserves

A contemporary image can later become evidence. Preserving authorship, the circumstances of its making, the date, the place and the connected story is therefore part of the work itself.

6

Responsibility in a leadership portrait

The executive portrait makes visible how a person relates to responsibility, decision-making, openness and the organisation. It creates a sign of trust, not a status symbol.

7

What an empty space can say

An empty space, a forgotten object, a chair, a piece of clothing or a personal trace can carry a story even when the person is not in the frame. Absence is not the opposite of presence — it is one of its strongest forms.

8

How the setting changes an image

The same image means something different as an artwork, a LinkedIn portrait, a press photograph, on social media, or inside AI systems. Context and metadata are part of the visual meaning.

9

Keeping the work findable

The work can remain part of our shared memory only if people can find it, understand where it came from and follow the stories around it. Keeping the archive clear and connected is part of caring for the photographs.

Working method

How I work

From the first conversation to the finished archive, these are the eight simple steps I follow for portraits, exhibitions and commissioned work.

Explore the eight-step method
1

Getting to know the story

Understanding the person, the story, the organisation, the situation of use, and the later life of the image.

2

Recognising the roles

Observing which expectations the sitter is trying to meet, and which gestures come from genuine presence.

3

Space and light

Location and lighting are not style for its own sake. Their task is to support the person, not to conceal them.

4

Trust

The raw material of portrait photography is not light — it is trust. The technical system must be stable enough that it never disturbs the human connection.

5

The unposed moment

The decisive image is often made the moment a pose stops feeling like a pose.

6

Choosing the photographs

Choosing an image is never a purely aesthetic decision. Together, the photographs tell a story and shape how it will be remembered.

7

Where the image will be used

A different image works as an artwork, on a cover, on a corporate page, in press material or on a social platform. I choose the photograph according to where and how people will see it.

8

Credits and safekeeping

File name, metadata, source, connected text, licence and a stable URL are all part of preserving the work.

Career arc

How it took shape

This is not a row of isolated dates. Each period shows what happened, which books or exhibitions grew from it, and how the work entered public reception.

Explore the 9-stage career arc

01 · 1979–1998

Family and visual background

I was born in Budapest. On my mother’s side, painting, design, handcraft and photography were present across several generations. This did not give me a ready-made artistic career, but it made visual thinking feel natural.

No artificial external evidence is retroactively claimed for this early period.

02 · 1999–2006

The beginning of digital image-making and documentation

I took part in the MOL Y2K project as an IT specialist. Digital image-making entered my life there: on my own initiative, I began photographing the project with a basic 1.3-megapixel digital camera. I later worked in information technology and telecommunications at the Hungarian Defence Forces Command for Communications and Information Systems while documenting internal events. The beginnings of HIPStudio grew from this combination of technical and documentary experience.

No artificial external evidence is retroactively claimed for this early period.

Author recollection

03 · 2006–2013

Among people — the formation of a portrait practice

HIPStudio continued independently. We documented clubs, radio stations, automotive events and occasions at the Hungaroring. Nights that lasted until dawn, rapidly changing situations and tens of thousands of human encounters taught me that a good portrait begins not with a face, but with trust.
