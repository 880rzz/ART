Curatorial dossier

The Anatomy of Presence

Visual anthropology and contemporary memory — the oeuvre of Norbert Bánhalmi

A note from me

If you are a curator, a director or a collector reading this: welcome, and thank you for the time. What follows is the short version of since 1999 — written to save you an afternoon. If any of it starts a conversation, that is exactly what it is for. My inbox is open: hello@norbertbanhalmi.com

The short version

My work sits somewhere between radical purism and dignified documentarism. The arc runs from the strict structures of military discipline to the sacred chaos of tantric intimacy — which sounds absurd written down, but that is honestly the road. These are not only aesthetic objects; they are case studies about identity, about how memory frays, and about repairing our relationship with the body. Three projects hold the whole thing up: Milestones ’56, Awakening, and The World of Woman. The work moves along a New York–Vienna–Budapest axis and is also shown internationally on Saatchi Art.

1 · From discipline to soul

Military precision as a foundation

In 2006 I founded the first professional photo studio of the Hungarian Defence Forces. That is not a biographical footnote — it is the key to how I work. Military photography demands objectivity, speed and technical perfection. I simply moved those skills somewhere else: the crosshairs now hold the movements of a human soul. My pictures live on the tension between order and chaos. The subjects touch the deepest vulnerability — illness, ageing, trauma — while the execution stays surgical and quiet.

New York: the school of seeing

New York is where my eye finally settled. There I learned to see, not just to look. The urban years also produced Sunrise Ride, which won the audience award at the European Environment Agency's international competition, and taught me how to lift a documentary moment to fine-art level.

Radical purism

Since Real Women (30+1) in 2014 I have held one position against the retouching age: wrinkles, skin texture and asymmetry are not flaws but the authenticating seals of a life. I light naturally or softly, and I take the social mask off before I take the picture.

2 · Historical trauma: Milestones ’56

Made for the 60th anniversary with the Hungarian House in New York — the strongest example of me working as a historian more than an artist. Months of fieldwork across New York State, Connecticut and New Jersey; oral-history interviews; and a diptych concept in which survivors are photographed together with the object they carried out of Hungary. Classical portraiture, Rembrandt light, direct gazes, no victim poses. The project also triggered a genuine historiographical argument about the categories of hero and perpetrator — which, curatorially, only raises its value: I recorded rather than judged, and that raw memory is what real trauma processing requires.

Milestones ’56 →

3 · The body as battlefield and sanctuary: Awakening

Realised with Semmelweis University oncology (Dr. Magdolna Dank) and psychiatrist Dr. Imre Csernus — an institutional interlacing of art and medicine. Where contemporary culture treats cancer as taboo or shock imagery, I focused on regeneration. For the participants the sessions worked as active therapy. Monochrome conversion and sculptural light integrate the scars into the harmony of the body as decorations of survival; poems by Vincent & Vincent act as emotional resonators. The series toured Budapest, Noszvaj and Veszprém and hangs permanently at the Csolnoky Ferenc Hospital Oncology Centre.

Awakening — The New Beginning →

4 · The stations of female existence: The World of Woman

Nine natural transitions, with writer Miklós Vámos — a visual revolt against the young-thin-perfect triangle. Recognising the limits of a static exhibition, I opened the project into participatory territory: an online space where anyone could submit her own story, which moved me from auteur to facilitator. Technically it showcases my minimalism — a small camera rather than an intimidating DSLR, so eye contact survives and physical proximity becomes emotional proximity. The Stefánia Palace finale added a roundtable with coaches and models: art out of the white cube and into social forum.

5 · The technologist artist

  • OM SYSTEM Ambassador (2018–) — The Japan Times covered how I renewed the brand's communication in Hungary around user experience, education and community rather than specifications.
  • First Hungarian photographic NFT collection — With The Men's Dream I was the first to bring Hungarian photographic tradition onto the blockchain, debuting with a charity auction in a Manhattan club.
  • Rege Gallery, Tihany (2020–) — Co-founder and curator of a digital exhibition space built with Samsung — sacred space fused with profane technology.
  • Sunrise Ride NFT — The EEA award-winning image later paired digital ownership with a physical artwork and an original musical composition.

6 · Toward sacred intimacy: Touch

The 2024 Touch Vienna and Touch Munich projects turn toward spirituality: tantra massage as holistic care rather than objectified body. The earlier monochrome yields to gold tones and candlelight. In societies where skin hunger has become endemic, rehabilitating touch felt like the most useful thing photography could do.

7 · Why this might interest you

  • Authentic narrative — A deeply humanist artist arriving from a military background — a story the works substantiate rather than decorate.
  • Social embeddedness — Not l'art pour l'art: emigration, illness, female roles, historical memory.
  • Visual consistency — Radical purism and monochrome give the oeuvre a unified, recognisable identity.
  • Technical innovation — NFT and digital-exhibition experience speaks to younger collectors and technology-oriented galleries.

Exhibition concepts I would love to build with you

  • Scars and Memory — Milestones ’56 and Awakening in parallel — the visual analogy of historical and biological wounds.
  • The Sacred Touch — Touch Vienna with I Became a Father — the spectrum of intimacy, parental to spiritual.
  • Faces of Resilience — A survey portrait exhibition from the refugees of ’56 to the survivors of cancer.
  • EUFÓRIA — The Anatomy of Presence (2027) — Capa's legacy rewritten for our decade: twenty unstaged portraits at the border of joy, freedom, pain and absolution.

Curatorial framework

The oeuvre as five connected periods

These periods are not separate careers. They describe how the same central enquiry — when a person, memory or community becomes genuinely present — moved through different subjects, places and public forms.

I · 1999–2013

Discipline, role and constructed identity

The early practice grew from commissioned and institutional environments. The central tension was already visible: the difference between an assigned role and the person who remains present inside it. Structure, restraint and responsibility became lasting working principles.

II · 2014–2016

Human stories and historical memory

New York, female identity, fatherhood and the portraits connected to 1956 shifted the work toward personal testimony. Photography became a way to hold individual experience and public history in the same frame without reducing either to illustration.

III · 2017–2018

The body as memory, illness and recovery

Awakening, Snippets and The World of Woman focused on the body after change: illness, recovery, intimacy, ageing and turning points. Images, testimony, literature and performance were used together, while each person retained authorship over their own experience.

IV · 2019–2022

Institutions, publishing and curatorial responsibility

Books, exhibitions, ambassadorship and gallery work placed the practice inside cultural and technological systems. The question became how an artwork can remain human and credible while moving through institutions, public presentation and new forms of circulation.

V · 2023–present

Community, education and public presence

Vienna, community building, teaching, charity projects and executive portraiture extended the enquiry into relationships. Presence here means trust, responsibility and the ability to make another person visible without turning them into a visual stereotype.

Research thesis

The anatomy of presence

The central question of the photographic practice: when does a person become genuinely present in an image? Presence is not the same as standing physically in front of the camera. I examine how it forms along nine dimensions — the thread connecting the intimate portraits, the historical documentation, the books and the executive portraits.

1

Physical presence

The body, the gaze, posture, hands, breath, gesture, and the relationship between light and space. The body is not a formal prop; it carries experience.

2

Psychological presence

The moment when the sitter is no longer performing an expected role. The portrait is not an exposure but a safe situation in which defences can briefly be set down.

3

Relational presence

The trust built between photographer and sitter. The quality of an image is not only an optical or technical outcome — it is a consequence of the human encounter.

4

Social presence

A portrait can also be the document of a community, a social situation or an era. A personal image can therefore reach beyond the individual.

5

Historical presence

A contemporary image can later become evidence. Preserving authorship, the circumstances of its making, the date, the place and the connected story is therefore part of the work itself.

6

Leadership presence

The executive portrait makes visible how a person relates to responsibility, decision-making, openness and the organisation. It creates a sign of trust, not a status symbol.

7

The presence of absence

An empty space, a forgotten object, a chair, a piece of clothing or a personal trace can carry a story even when the person is not in the frame. Absence is not the opposite of presence — it is one of its strongest forms.

8

Digital presence

The same image means something different as an artwork, a LinkedIn portrait, a press photograph, on social media, or inside AI systems. Context and metadata are part of the visual meaning.

9

Archival presence

An oeuvre remains culturally present only if it is searchable, sourced and interconnected. The entity-first archive is therefore not a technical side project — it is the long-term preservation of presence.

This thesis is what turns EUFÓRIA — The Anatomy of Presence into a research programme →

Working method

The creative method

From the encounter to the long-term life of the image — eight steps I follow the same way in every portrait, exhibition and executive commission.

1

Preliminary understanding

Understanding the person, the story, the organisation, the situation of use, and the later life of the image.

2

Recognising the roles

Observing which expectations the sitter is trying to meet, and which gestures come from genuine presence.

3

Space and light

Location and lighting are not style for its own sake. Their task is to support the person, not to conceal them.

4

Trust

The raw material of portrait photography is not light — it is trust. The technical system must be stable enough that it never disturbs the human connection.

5

The unposed moment

The decisive image is often made the moment a pose stops feeling like a pose.

6

Selection and sequence

Choosing an image is never a purely aesthetic decision. Images together build narrative, reputation and memory.

7

Context and use

A different image works as an artwork, on a cover, on a corporate page, in press material or on a social platform. Visual strategy manages the entire system of use.

8

Authorship and archiving

File name, metadata, source, connected text, licence and a stable URL are all part of preserving the work.

Whole-oeuvre structure

One investigation, several distinct periods

The archive is organised around a single long-term inquiry rather than around isolated books, exhibitions or commissions. An early, self-initiated corporate photography assignment is the point of departure. From there, each period changes the social setting, the bodies and the public context, while the central question remains the same: when does a person, a memory or a community become genuinely present?

I

Corporate beginnings — point of departure

The first coherent field of inquiry: discipline, role, institutional identity and the tension between appearance and truth.

II

New York, memory and public testimony

New York, women’s stories, fatherhood and the portraits connected to 1956 move the inquiry toward personal testimony and lived memory.

III

The body as memory and recovery

Awakening, Snippets and The World of Woman examine the body after illness, intimacy, identity and turning points.

IV

Institutions, teaching and communities

Books, exhibitions, curatorial work, education and community-building make presence a shared cultural practice.

V

Public and leadership presence

Executive and public portraiture apply the same artistic discipline to trust, responsibility and visible leadership.

Books, exhibitions, press records, moving-image documents and community projects are evidence attached to these periods. They belong to one body of work, not to separate careers.
