EUFÓRIA — The Anatomy of Presence

20 people · 20 found moments · the cycle is complete only when all twenty works exist

What is EUFÓRIA?

EUFÓRIA is a photographic project about twenty people of Hungarian origin and twenty found moments. I look for the point where a public role gives way to something personal — in a gesture, a gaze or an unguarded movement.

EUFÓRIA is in development. The exhibition has no confirmed venue or date; it remains planned until all twenty works are made and documented.

Why it began

The concept

“Photography, for me, is not documentation but a state of being. A state of mind. Presence. Pure force.”

There is a duality that has run through my whole life. I am an applied photographer — and an artist. Commissions, deadlines, briefs on one side; inner visions, silences, questions on the other. For years those two worlds ran in parallel, sometimes against each other, sometimes feeding each other. Today I know I never had to choose. That tension made me who I am, and EUFÓRIA is where both finally point in the same direction. Not a closure. An arrival.

The idea came from my muse. She is the one who walked this road with me and believed in what I do even when I did not quite believe it myself. Her passion is dance. When she dances something breaks free — in her, and in the room. Energies move that can shake the saddest person awake and pull them back into life. I have lived through that state beside her more times than I can count. Only now do I understand that I am ready to create inside that vibration. Not to shape it. Not to filter it. To give it back, unveiled.

How I make the pictures

I look for moments that cannot be directed or repeated. In a shared session, conversation and trust make room for them; at a public event, I work with the situation as it unfolds. The image enters the project when the person, the moment and my attention come together.

What presence means here

Here, presence means the point at which a person becomes more than the role we recognise. The project connects the body, identity and public life without reducing a portrait to an illustration of status.

This will not be a classical photography exhibition but a total work of art — image, music, technology and human stories in one space. Twenty works. I do not begin with a list of people: I look for moments. A person enters EUFÓRIA when the person, the situation and my photographic presence converge strongly enough to become an autonomous work. The exhibition remains a plan until all twenty moments have been found, photographed and documented.

Work Nº 1

Péter Magyar — Heroes’ Square, Budapest, 15 March 2026, 16:57

The first work places the project’s question inside a public gathering: one face, one gaze and one found moment at Heroes’ Square.

Péter Magyar: background and Wikimedia records

The person and the moment

Péter Magyar · Heroes’ Square

The moment

I made this portrait at Heroes’ Square in Budapest on 15 March 2026 at 16:57, while Péter Magyar was speaking at a public gathering. I kept one face and one gaze from a much larger scene. That concentrated encounter became the first publicly released work of EUFÓRIA.

Why it belongs in EUFÓRIA

The photograph brings the project’s question into public life: how can one face remain a human presence under the weight of a familiar public role? This is an artistic reading of the image, not a claim about the subject’s inner state.

The picture’s own life

When I released the portrait under CC BY-SA 4.0, I made it possible for others to reuse it. It travelled into Wikimedia, Wikipedia, Wikidata and editorial publications. From then on, I no longer chose every context in which it would be seen.

Azahriah: background and Wikimedia records

The person

Azahriah · Attila Baukó

Azahriah (Attila Baukó) is a Hungarian singer-songwriter and performer whose rise from online creator to arena-scale artist became one of the defining stories of contemporary Hungarian popular culture. In 2024 he became the first Hungarian artist to fill the Puskás Arena on three consecutive nights; 138,890 tickets were sold across the three concerts. Contemporary cultural coverage interpreted the unprecedented run not only as a music-industry record, but also as evidence of a new generation taking visible possession of the country's largest cultural spaces.
